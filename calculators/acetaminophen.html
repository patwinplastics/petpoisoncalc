Acetaminophen (Tylenol) Calculator

Estimate how urgently your dog or cat needs veterinary attention after eating acetaminophen (Tylenol, paracetamol), based on species, weight, tablet strength, and count.

Cats: any amount of acetaminophen is an emergency.

Cats cannot break down acetaminophen safely, and signs have been reported at 10 mg/kg, a fraction of one regular tablet for an average cat. Dogs tolerate more, but higher doses damage the liver and red blood cells. This tool estimates relative urgency using published veterinary toxicology data. It cannot rule out risk, and every ingestion should be evaluated by a veterinarian or animal poison control.

Emergency vet or poison control: which do I call?

An antidote (acetylcysteine) and supportive care work best when started early, before signs appear.

Go straight to an emergency vet if…

  • The pet is a cat, regardless of amount
  • Your pet has brown or muddy-colored gums, trouble breathing, a swollen face or paws, or is weak
  • The estimated dose for your dog is in the Emergency range
  • Poison control or your vet has told you to come in

Call poison control first if…

  • Your dog just ate the tablets and seems normal
  • You are unsure of the strength or how many were eaten
  • The product was a combination medicine with other ingredients
  • You want a case number to hand to your vet
Never give a pet acetaminophen or any human pain reliever unless a veterinarian prescribes it. Do not induce vomiting at home unless told to.

How acetaminophen poisoning develops

Acetaminophen damages red blood cells (methemoglobinemia, which reduces oxygen delivery) and the liver. Cats mostly develop blood changes; dogs more often develop liver injury.

1 – 4 hours

Blood changes (especially cats)

  • Brown or muddy gums
  • Fast or labored breathing
  • Weakness, lethargy
  • Swelling of the face or paws
12 – 36 hours

Liver injury (especially dogs)

  • Vomiting, loss of appetite
  • Abdominal pain
  • Liver values rise on bloodwork
24 – 72 hours

Advanced signs

  • Yellow gums or eyes (jaundice)
  • Collapse
  • Death without treatment in severe cases
Repeated doses over several days can cause toxicity at lower amounts than a single dose. Tell your vet if acetaminophen was given more than once.

Published case data behind these estimates

These figures come from the Merck Veterinary Manual and an ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center review. They describe documented clinical thresholds, not a line between "safe" and "dangerous."

Dose Documented clinical effect
Cats: 10 mg/kg Clinical signs reported in some cats; no safe dose for cats
Cats: 40–50 mg/kg Toxicosis generally occurs
Dogs: > 100 mg/kg Clinical signs, liver injury possible
Dogs: > 200 mg/kg Methemoglobinemia reported

This calculator uses: cats = Emergency at any amount. Dogs: under 50 mg/kg = contact a professional now; 50 to under 100 mg/kg = Urgent (a conservative safety margin below the 100 mg/kg threshold, since repeated doses and individual sensitivity lower the toxic dose; this margin is our choice, not a published cutoff); 100 mg/kg and above = Emergency.
