Xylitol can cause dangerous hypoglycemia within 30 minutes.

Unlike many other toxic ingestions, xylitol acts fast. Clinical signs of low blood sugar can appear in as little as 30 minutes, and liver injury can develop separately at higher doses. This tool estimates relative urgency using published veterinary toxicology data. It cannot rule out risk, and every ingestion should be evaluated by a veterinarian or animal poison control immediately, not just when convenient.

Emergency vet or poison control: which do I call?

Because xylitol acts so quickly, speed matters more here than with most other toxic ingestions. In most cases you should use both resources.

Go straight to an emergency vet if…

  • Your dog is already weak, wobbly, vomiting, trembling, or collapsed, these can be signs of dangerously low blood sugar
  • It has been more than 30–60 minutes since ingestion, since hypoglycemia can develop very quickly
  • The product was a peanut butter, baked good, or pure xylitol source, which tend to carry higher doses per serving
  • A poison control consultation or your vet has told you to bring the dog in

Call poison control first if…

  • Your dog just ate the item seconds or minutes ago and seems otherwise normal
  • You are unsure how much xylitol was actually in the product
  • You need help deciding how urgently to act for your dog's specific weight
  • You want a case number and treatment plan you can hand directly to your regular or emergency vet
Because xylitol can cause hypoglycemia within 30 minutes, do not wait to see if your dog seems fine. Call while heading toward a vet rather than waiting at home to observe symptoms.

How xylitol toxicosis develops

Xylitol triggers a much stronger insulin release in dogs than in humans, causing blood sugar to crash rapidly. A separate, less understood mechanism can cause liver injury at higher doses, sometimes without severe hypoglycemia first.

30 minutes – a few hours

Hypoglycemia (low blood sugar)

  • Vomiting
  • Weakness or lethargy
  • Loss of coordination, stumbling
  • Tremors or seizures in severe cases
  • Collapse
4 – 12 hours

Liver enzyme changes

  • Increases in liver enzymes are often detectable on bloodwork during this window
  • May occur even in dogs that did not show hypoglycemia symptoms
24 – 48 hours or longer

Liver injury and failure

  • Clinical signs of liver injury may not appear until this point at higher doses
  • Can progress to acute liver failure, particularly at doses above 500 mg/kg
  • Hospitalization and monitoring are typically required through this window
Some sugar-free gums release xylitol more slowly, which can delay hypoglycemia by up to 12–18 hours in rare cases. A delayed or absent early reaction does not mean the danger has passed.

Published case data behind these estimates

These figures come from the Merck Veterinary Manual and peer-reviewed case reports. They describe documented clinical thresholds, not a line between "safe" and "dangerous."

Xylitol dose Documented clinical effect
> 100 mg/kg body weight (45 mg/lb) Associated with hypoglycemia (low blood sugar)
> 500 mg/kg body weight (227 mg/lb) May result in severe hepatic (liver) insufficiency or failure
Product type Typical xylitol content (approx.)
Sugar-free gum or mints ≈ 0.3–1 g per piece
Xylitol peanut butter ≈ 5–13 g per 100 g
Baked goods / candy Varies widely, always check the label
Pure xylitol / baking substitute 100% xylitol by weight

Xylitol content varies significantly between brands and products. Always check the ingredient label when possible, and when in doubt, choose the higher-risk category for a more conservative estimate.
