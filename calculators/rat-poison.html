Did your dog eat rat or mouse poison? Here is what to do.

If your dog ate rat or mouse poison, treat it as an emergency. Use this page to identify the poison type from the package and know what to expect.

Last reviewed October 15, 2026 by PetPoisonCalc against the sources listed below.

Any rodenticide ingestion is an emergency. Do not wait for signs.

Rat and mouse poisons are designed to kill mammals, and the most dangerous types cause no signs for hours to days, by which time treatment is harder. You cannot tell the poison type by bait color or shape. Bring the package (or a photo of the active ingredient) to the vet. This page does not calculate a dose: because bait concentration varies and the margin of safety is narrow, every ingestion is treated as an emergency.

Emergency vet or poison control: which do I call?

Early decontamination (inducing vomiting and activated charcoal) within the first few hours is the most effective treatment for most rodenticides. A vet should do this.

Go straight to an emergency vet if…

  • Always, as soon as possible, for any rodenticide ingestion
  • Your dog is bleeding, coughing, weak, has pale gums, tremors, seizures, or is drinking and urinating more
  • You do not know the active ingredient
  • Your dog may have eaten a poisoned rodent

Call poison control first if…

  • Call on the way to the vet so the clinic is ready
  • Have the package or the EPA registration number ready
  • Ask for a case number to hand to the vet
  • Never wait at home to see whether signs develop
Do not induce vomiting at home, especially with zinc or aluminum phosphide: vomiting can release toxic phosphine gas that can harm people nearby. If phosphide is possible, drive to the vet with the windows open.

What to expect by poison type

Each class of rodenticide works differently. Some act within hours, others take days to show signs.

1 – 4 hours

Zinc / aluminum phosphide

  • Vomiting (may smell like garlic or rotten fish)
  • Restlessness, trouble breathing, collapse
Hours – 7 days

Bromethalin

  • High doses: tremors, seizures within hours to 36 hours
  • Lower doses: back-leg weakness or paralysis within 1–5 days
  • No antidote
18 – 72 hours

Cholecalciferol (vitamin D3)

  • Vomiting, loss of appetite, increased thirst and urination
  • Rising calcium damages kidneys; weeks of monitoring may be needed
  • No antidote
2 – 5 days

Anticoagulants

  • Usually no signs at first
  • Then bleeding: weakness, pale gums, coughing, nosebleeds, bruising, blood in urine or stool
  • Vitamin K1 is the antidote
Because bromethalin and cholecalciferol products have become more common in the US, never assume a poison is an anticoagulant without checking the package.

Published case data behind these estimates

These facts come from the Merck Veterinary Manual. Doses are not used here because bait concentrations vary and toxic doses can be very low.

Poison type Key facts
Anticoagulants Clotting problems develop within 2–5 days; vitamin K1 is the antidote; second-generation products (brodifacoum, bromadiolone) are toxic after a single feeding
Bromethalin Nerve toxin causing brain swelling; signs within 1–7 days; no antidote; early decontamination is key
Cholecalciferol High calcium and phosphorus within 72 hours, kidney damage; no antidote; narrow margin of safety
Zinc / aluminum phosphide Releases phosphine gas in the stomach; rapid onset; risk to people from vomit

Common questions

Why does this page not calculate a dose?

Bait strength varies, and the margin of safety is narrow. Some of the most dangerous rodenticides cause no signs for hours or days. This page helps you identify the active ingredient and what to expect. Any ingestion is an emergency, including a case where your dog only might have eaten bait.

Can I tell the poison type by the color of the bait?

No. Color, shape, and flavor do not tell you the active ingredient. Read the package for the chemical name, or bring the package or a clear photo. Do not assume the bait is an older anticoagulant product.

Should I make my dog vomit after rat poison?

Do not induce vomiting at home. That is especially dangerous if the bait may be zinc phosphide or aluminum phosphide, because vomiting can release phosphine gas that harms people nearby. If a phosphide is possible, drive to the clinic with the windows open and do not try home treatment.
