Did your dog or cat eat aspirin? Check how much is toxic.

Estimate how urgently your dog or cat needs veterinary attention after eating aspirin (Bayer, Bufferin, Ecotrin, low-dose "baby" aspirin, store-brand acetylsalicylic acid), based on species, body weight, tablet strength, and how many tablets were eaten.

Last reviewed October 28, 2026 by PetPoisonCalc against the sources listed below.

Cats are far more sensitive to aspirin than dogs.

Cats clear aspirin very slowly (its half-life in cats is about 37.5 hours, per the Merck Veterinary Manual), so this calculator treats any aspirin a cat eats as an emergency. Dogs tolerate aspirin better, but it can still cause vomiting, stomach ulcers, a dangerous acid imbalance, and seizures at high doses. Older pets, pets with kidney, liver, or stomach problems, and pets that also take another NSAID or a steroid can be harmed at lower doses than listed here. This tool estimates relative urgency using published veterinary toxicology data. It cannot rule out risk, and every ingestion should be evaluated by a veterinarian or animal poison control. Products like Pepto-Bismol, oil of wintergreen, and muscle-rub creams also contain salicylates; this calculator does not cover them, so call poison control.

Emergency vet or poison control: which do I call?

Treatment works best when started early, often before any signs appear. If the tablets were eaten recently, vets commonly induce vomiting and give activated charcoal, then use stomach protectants and IV fluids. Severe cases may need treatment for an acid imbalance. There is no antidote for aspirin, so early decontamination matters.

Go straight to an emergency vet if…

  • Your pet is vomiting (especially blood), panting hard, weak, wobbly, very hot, or having a seizure
  • Your cat ate any aspirin
  • The estimated dose is in the Emergency range
  • Your pet is older, has kidney, liver, or stomach problems, or also takes another NSAID or a steroid

Call poison control first if…

  • Your dog just ate the tablets and seems normal
  • You are unsure of the tablet strength or how many were eaten
  • The product was a combination medicine (Excedrin, some cold and migraine products) or a salicylate cream, Pepto-Bismol, or oil of wintergreen
  • You want a case number and treatment plan to hand to your vet
Do not try to make your pet vomit at home unless a veterinarian or poison control tells you to, and never give any other pain medicine, including Pepto-Bismol, which also contains a salicylate.

How aspirin poisoning develops

Aspirin blocks the prostaglandins that protect the stomach lining and also affects platelets, so bleeding can be prolonged. At high doses it disrupts how cells make energy, which can cause fever, fast breathing, and a serious acid imbalance. Aspirin is absorbed quickly; blood levels peak about 0.5 to 3 hours after ingestion (Merck Veterinary Manual).

Within hours

Stomach and gut signs

  • Vomiting, sometimes with blood
  • Loss of appetite, listlessness, weakness
  • Dogs may vomit within about 2 hours
Hours to a day

Higher doses: whole-body effects

  • Fast or heavy breathing, panting, fever
  • Acid imbalance found on bloodwork
  • Seizures or coma after very large doses
Days later

Ulcer and bleeding complications

  • Black, tarry stool or pale gums
  • Prolonged bleeding from small wounds
  • Liver injury in some severe cases
Cats clear aspirin far more slowly than dogs, so effects in cats can last longer. Follow-up bloodwork is commonly recommended after a significant ingestion.

Published case data behind these estimates

These figures come from the Merck Veterinary Manual and a veterinary critical care textbook chapter on salicylate toxicity. Sources agree that the exact toxic dose of aspirin is not clear and varies between individual animals, so this calculator uses the lowest reported levels as its cutoffs. They describe documented clinical effects, not a line between "safe" and "dangerous."

Aspirin dose Documented clinical effect
Dogs: 50 mg/kg Stomach and gut signs alone may occur (Small Animal Critical Care Medicine, Salicylates chapter). At 50 mg/kg every 12 hours, dogs vomited about 2 hours after each dose (IVIS Veterinary Toxicology).
Dogs: 100 to 500 mg/kg Severe signs of toxicity reported across this wide range; toxicity may result from an acute ingestion of 100 mg/kg (Small Animal Critical Care Medicine)
Dogs: 450 mg/kg A single ingestion can cause stomach upset, high body temperature, panting, seizures, or coma (Merck Veterinary Manual)
Dogs: 25 to 50 mg/kg, repeated Stomach erosions and ulcers developed with repeated dosing over days to weeks (Merck Veterinary Manual)
Cats: over 50 mg/kg Toxicosis may occur; any exposure over 100 mg/kg should be considered serious and potentially lethal (Small Animal Critical Care Medicine). Cats have reacted to much smaller repeated doses (Merck Veterinary Manual).

This calculator uses, for dogs: under 50 mg/kg = contact a professional now; 50 to under 100 mg/kg = Urgent; 100 mg/kg and above = Emergency. If your dog is older, has kidney, liver, or stomach problems, or takes another NSAID or a steroid, any dose of 50 mg/kg or more is shown as Emergency. For cats, any amount is shown as Emergency, because no precise safe threshold has been published and cats clear aspirin very slowly. There is no published acute dose below which aspirin is known to cause no harm, so even the lowest result still says to call.

Common questions

How much aspirin is toxic to a dog?

Veterinary references report stomach upset and vomiting in dogs from about 50 mg of aspirin per kg of body weight, and severe poisoning across a wide range of about 100 to 500 mg per kg. One 325 mg regular aspirin is about 72 mg per kg for a 10 lb dog and about 24 mg per kg for a 30 lb dog. Call poison control or your vet for any amount.

Is aspirin more dangerous for cats?

Yes. Cats clear aspirin very slowly (its half-life in cats is about 37 hours) and have reacted to small doses. This calculator treats any aspirin a cat eats as an emergency.

My dog ate one baby aspirin. What should I do?

One 81 mg low-dose tablet is a small amount for most dogs, but call your vet or a poison control line to confirm, especially for a small dog, an older dog, or a dog that takes another NSAID or a steroid. Do not give any other pain reliever.
