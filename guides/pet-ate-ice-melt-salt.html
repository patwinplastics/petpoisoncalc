My Dog or Cat Ate Ice Melt, Rock Salt, or Salt Dough: What to Do Now

A torn bag of ice melt in the garage, a dog licking salty paws after a walk, a salt dough ornament knocked off the tree: winter brings a lot of salt within reach of pets. Most licks cause only an upset stomach, but larger amounts can cause salt poisoning, which can lead to seizures. Here is how to tell the difference and what to do.

If your pet ate ice melt, rock salt, or salt dough: ASPCA Animal Poison Control (888) 426-4435 Pet Poison Helpline (855) 764-7661 Find a 24/7 emergency vet near me Both hotlines are open 24/7. If your dog or cat ate ice melt, rock salt, table salt, homemade play dough, or a salt dough ornament, call an animal poison control center or your vet now, with the bag or recipe in hand. Go straight to an emergency vet if your pet is vomiting repeatedly, wobbly, shaking, or having a seizure, or if your pet is a cat. Use the salt and ice melt calculator to estimate the dose.

What to do right now

  1. Move your pet away from the bag, the treated area, or the dough, and pick up what is left.
  2. Wipe the mouth, paws, and belly fur with a damp cloth so your pet cannot lick off more. If your pet rolled in ice melt or put its face in the bag, ASPCA advises a bath.
  3. Offer fresh water. Do not force your pet to drink.
  4. Read the ingredients on the bag, or take a photo, and note the time.
  5. Call ASPCA Animal Poison Control or Pet Poison Helpline, or your vet.
  6. Do not make your pet vomit unless a professional tells you to, and never use salt or salt water to do it. That can cause salt poisoning on its own (Pet Poison Helpline).

How much salt is toxic?

Salt is sodium chloride. When a pet eats a lot at once, sodium in the blood rises quickly and pulls water out of brain cells. The Merck Veterinary Manual says excess salt makes dogs vomit within several hours, and signs can progress to weakness, diarrhea, muscle tremors, and seizures.

Dogs: signs of salt poisoning can appear after about 2 to 3 g of salt per kg of body weight, and the acute lethal dose is about 4 g per kg (Merck Veterinary Manual; Pet Poison Helpline).

Cats: there is no published toxic dose for cats. ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center notes that dogs and cats that eat a lot of sodium chloride ice melt usually vomit and can develop high blood sodium, and cats are small, so PetPoisonCalc treats any amount a cat swallows as an emergency.

Not all ice melts are the same

Pet Poison Helpline and the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center list these common ice melt ingredients. Check the bag, because many products are blends.

  • Sodium chloride (rock salt, halite): the most common. Small amounts cause stomach upset; large amounts can cause salt poisoning.
  • Calcium chloride, calcium carbonate, calcium magnesium acetate (CMA): Pet Poison Helpline calls calcium salts the most hazardous because they are the most severe irritants, to the stomach and to skin and paws.
  • Potassium chloride: a severe irritant that can cause vomiting or diarrhea with blood.
  • Magnesium chloride: stomach upset; very large amounts can raise blood magnesium, mainly in pets with kidney disease.
  • Urea: often sold as "pet safe." Usually drooling and mild stomach upset, but large amounts can cause weakness, tremors, and methemoglobinemia, a blood problem that reduces oxygen delivery.

There is no published toxic dose for the non-sodium ingredients, so the calculator treats any amount of them as an emergency. Pet Poison Helpline's bottom line: all ice melts have the potential to be hazardous.

Salt dough ornaments and homemade play dough

Salt dough is flour, water, and a lot of salt. Today's Veterinary Nurse notes some homemade play dough recipes hold about 8 g of salt per tablespoon, so a single tablespoon is close to 2 g per kg for a 10 lb dog. Baked ornaments have lost their water, so they are even saltier by weight, and dogs often treat them like a salty biscuit. ASPCA warns that even a small amount of salt dough could be dangerous, including ornaments that are years old.

Dogs vs cats: what is different

  • How they get it: dogs tend to eat ice melt from the bag, lick it off sidewalks, or eat snow with de-icer in it. Cats more often swallow it while grooming treated paws and fur, so it can happen without anyone noticing.
  • Dose data: the 2 to 4 g per kg figures come from dogs. No toxic dose has been published for cats, so treat any amount as an emergency for a cat.
  • Size: most cats weigh under 12 lb, so the same spoonful is a much bigger dose than it is for most dogs.
  • Paws: both species can get dry, cracked, or burned paw pads from ice melt, especially calcium chloride. Wipe paws after every walk or trip through the garage.

Symptoms and timeline

  • Within about 3 hours: vomiting (often the first sign), drooling, diarrhea, heavy thirst and peeing, not eating
  • Over the next hours: lethargy, wobbliness, disorientation, muscle tremors, fast heart rate and breathing
  • Large doses: seizures, collapse, coma, and death are possible
  • Ice melt irritation: red or sore mouth, pawing at the face, limping, licking or chewing at paws, cracked or red paw pads

Timing is from the Merck Veterinary Manual and Today's Veterinary Nurse. In ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center data, vomiting was the most common sign after ice melt exposure.

Go straight to an emergency vet if

  • Your pet is vomiting repeatedly, very thirsty, wobbly, shaking, confused, or having a seizure
  • Your pet is a cat and swallowed any salt, salt dough, or ice melt
  • Your pet ate a salt dough ornament or a chunk of homemade play dough
  • There is blood in vomit or stool, or sores in the mouth
  • The calculator shows an Emergency result

What the vet will do

If your pet ate a lot of salt recently and has no signs yet, the vet may make a dog vomit and then watch closely with water available. Pets with signs get blood tests for sodium and other electrolytes and IV fluids. The Merck Veterinary Manual stresses that sodium must be brought down slowly, over about 2 to 3 days, because lowering it too fast can cause the brain to swell. Seizures are treated with medicine. Activated charcoal does not bind salts well, so it is not usually helpful (ASPCA Toxicology Brief). For calcium, potassium, or magnesium ice melts, treatment is mostly for irritation: stomach protectants, fluids, and electrolyte checks. Today's Veterinary Nurse notes that pets with mild to moderate signs that get prompt treatment generally do well.

How to prevent it

  • Store ice melt in a closed bin or on a high shelf, not in an open bag on the garage floor.
  • Use as little ice melt as works, sweep up extra, and keep pets off freshly treated areas.
  • Wipe paws, legs, and belly with a damp cloth after walks. Booties or paw wax help some dogs.
  • Do not let dogs eat snow or slush along treated sidewalks and roads.
  • Hang salt dough ornaments out of reach, or skip them in homes with dogs, and store crafts in sealed boxes.
  • Keep homemade play dough put away and supervise kids and pets during craft time.
  • A "pet-friendly" ice melt can lower the risk, but it is still not safe to eat.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if a dog eats ice melt?

Most dogs that lick a little ice melt get drooling, vomiting, or diarrhea. Eating a lot of a sodium chloride ice melt can raise blood sodium and cause wobbliness, tremors, and seizures, and calcium or potassium chloride products can burn the mouth and stomach. Call poison control with the bag in hand.

How much rock salt is toxic to a dog?

The Merck Veterinary Manual says signs of salt poisoning can appear in dogs after about 2 to 3 g of salt per kg of body weight, and about 4 g per kg can be lethal. For a 20 lb dog, 2 g per kg is about 18 g, or roughly 1 tablespoon of salt.

Is ice melt dangerous for cats?

Yes. There is no published toxic dose of salt for cats, and cats groom ice melt off their paws and fur, so they can swallow it without you seeing. Calcium chloride can also irritate their paws and mouth. Treat any ice melt a cat swallows as an emergency.

What should I do if my dog ate a salt dough ornament?

Call your vet or ASPCA Animal Poison Control at (888) 426-4435 right away, even if your dog seems fine and even if the ornament is old. Salt dough can hold about 8 g of salt per tablespoon. Do not make your dog vomit unless a professional tells you to.

Is pet-safe ice melt really safe?

Not completely. Urea-based products are usually the ones labeled pet safe and are generally milder, but Pet Poison Helpline notes large amounts can still cause weakness, tremors, and blood changes, and all ice melts can upset the stomach and irritate paws. Use it sparingly and wipe paws after walks.
