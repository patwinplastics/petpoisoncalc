My Dog or Cat Ate Coffee Grounds, Caffeine Pills, or an Energy Drink: What to Do

Coffee grounds in the trash, a dropped caffeine pill, a guest's energy drink left on the coffee table: caffeine is easy to leave within reach, especially around the holidays. A lick of coffee is rarely a problem, but beans, grounds, and pills pack a lot of caffeine into a small bite. Here is how to tell the difference and what to do.

If your dog or cat ate coffee grounds or beans, a caffeine pill, or drank coffee, tea, soda, or an energy drink, call an animal poison control center or your vet now, with the package in hand. Go straight to an emergency vet if your pet is restless, panting hard, trembling, vomiting, has a racing heartbeat, swallowed a pill, or is a cat. Use the caffeine calculator to estimate the dose.

What to do right now

  1. Take away what is left and keep your pet calm and quiet. Excitement raises the heart rate.
  2. Check the package or cup for what it was and how much is missing. Note the time.
  3. Look at the label for the mg of caffeine, or take a photo of it.
  4. Call ASPCA Animal Poison Control or Pet Poison Helpline, or your vet.
  5. Do not make your pet vomit unless a professional tells you to.
  6. Go to an emergency vet if your pet is shaking, panting hard, vomiting, collapsing, or has a racing heartbeat.

How much caffeine is toxic?

Caffeine and the theobromine in chocolate are both methylxanthines, and the Merck Veterinary Manual gives one set of effects for them in dogs: mild signs such as vomiting, diarrhea, and extra thirst at about 20 mg per kg of body weight, heart rhythm problems at 40 to 50 mg per kg, and seizures at 60 mg per kg or more. Caffeine alone has a reported lethal dose of about 140 mg per kg (American College of Veterinary Pharmacists; Gugler and colleagues, 2013), but severe signs and death can happen at much lower doses.

No firm cutoff exists for cats. A published list gives toxic doses of 80 to 150 mg per kg for cats, and cats are somewhat more sensitive than dogs. Because most cats are small, treat any caffeine a cat swallows as an emergency.

Where caffeine hides, especially in winter

  • Coffee grounds and beans: used grounds in the trash or compost, single-serve coffee pods that get chewed open, whole beans, and holiday coffee gift sets.
  • Caffeine pills and powders: diet pills, wake-up tablets, pre-workout powder, and caffeinated gum, often in a guest's bag or a purse on the floor.
  • Drinks: energy drinks, cola, tea, and sweetened coffee drinks left on a low table. Check the label for xylitol or other additives too.
  • Chocolate-covered espresso beans: both caffeine and theobromine. Use the chocolate calculator as well.
  • Medicines: some migraine and headache medicines combine caffeine with acetaminophen or aspirin, which are dangerous on their own. Pet Poison Helpline lists migraine medications as a caffeine source. Check the Tylenol and aspirin calculators too.

Dogs vs cats: what is different

  • How they get it: dogs are the usual victims because they eat grounds from the trash, chew open pill bottles, and steal drinks. Cats eat caffeine less often because they are pickier.
  • Sensitivity: cats are somewhat more sensitive to methylxanthines than dogs (Gugler and colleagues, 2013).
  • Size: most cats weigh under 12 lb, so the same cup or pill is a much bigger dose. A toy dog is at the same risk.
  • Dose data: the 20, 40, and 60 mg per kg figures are from dogs. For cats, treat any amount as an emergency.

Symptoms and timeline

  • Within 1 to 2 hours: restlessness, hyperactivity, panting, vomiting, diarrhea, and drinking and peeing a lot.
  • Next few hours: a fast or uneven heartbeat, tremors, stiff or wobbly movement, and a high body temperature.
  • Large doses: seizures, collapse, and death if untreated.
  • How long: signs can last 12 to 36 hours (Pet Poison Helpline), because a dog clears caffeine slowly, with a half-life of about 4.5 hours (DeClementi, 2011).

Go straight to an emergency vet if

  • Your pet is shaking, twitching, panting hard, or cannot settle
  • Your pet has a racing or uneven heartbeat, collapses, or has a seizure
  • Your pet swallowed a caffeine pill, powder, or a mouthful of dry grounds or beans
  • Your pet is a cat or a toy-breed dog and ate more than a taste
  • The calculator shows Emergency

What the vet will do

If your pet ate caffeine recently and has no signs yet, the vet may make a dog vomit and give activated charcoal. Pets with signs get IV fluids, heart and temperature monitoring, and medicines to calm tremors, seizures, and a fast heart rate. That is why calling right away matters.

How to prevent it

  • Put coffee grounds, used pods, and tea bags in a lidded bin pets cannot open, and keep compost fenced off.
  • Store caffeine pills, powders, and coffee beans in a closed cabinet, never in a bag on the floor.
  • Tell holiday guests to keep purses, backpacks, and gym bags up high or in a closed room.
  • Keep drinks, especially energy drinks, off low tables and out of reach of jumping pets.
  • Hang up chocolate-covered espresso beans and coffee gift sets with other treats.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if a dog eats coffee grounds?

Dry grounds and beans are concentrated caffeine, about 280 to 570 mg per ounce. A small dog that eats a few spoonfuls can get restless, pant, vomit, and develop a racing heart within 1 to 2 hours, and a large amount can cause seizures. Call poison control with the package in hand.

How much caffeine is toxic to a dog?

The Merck Veterinary Manual lists mild signs at about 20 mg of methylxanthine per kg, heart effects at 40 to 50 mg per kg, and seizures at 60 mg per kg or more. A 10 lb dog (4.5 kg) reaches 40 mg per kg at about 181 mg of caffeine, roughly 9 fl oz of strong brewed coffee or a third of an ounce of coffee beans.

Is it dangerous if my dog licked a little coffee?

A lick is a very small dose and most dogs are fine, but a toy breed or a cat can be affected by more than a lick, and the strength of the drink matters. If it was more than a taste, or you do not know how much, call poison control. Watch for restlessness, panting, vomiting, or shaking for the next several hours.

Is caffeine more dangerous for cats?

Cats are somewhat more sensitive to caffeine than dogs, and most cats weigh under 12 lb, so the same cup is a larger dose. No firm cutoff has been set for cats, so treat any caffeine a cat swallows as an emergency. Cats eat caffeine less often because they are pickier.

Are caffeine pills and energy drinks worse than coffee?

Pills and powders are the most dangerous because one tablet can hold 100 to 200 mg or more, and Pet Poison Helpline notes that fatality is common when dogs eat caffeine pills. Energy drinks can hold as much caffeine as strong coffee and may also contain xylitol or other additives, so read the label and call poison control.
